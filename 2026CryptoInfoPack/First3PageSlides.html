1) What does a bank do today… and why do people trust it?

←→ to navigate
Banks are trusted bookkeepers (plus services).

Most of “moving money” is really updating records safely — inside the bank and across many banks.

Interest income Loans, mortgages, credit — earn spread over funding costs.
Non-interest income Payments, account fees, FX spreads, services.
Trading income Markets activities (FX, securities, derivatives) with controls.
  • Bookkeeping is the core Every transaction becomes a debit/credit entry in a controlled ledger.
  • Trust comes from controls Regulation, audits, separation of duties, capital/liquidity, and governance.
  • It gets hard across multiple banks Each institution has its own books → reconciliation, delays, and operational cost.
A simple mental model

When value moves across banks, it’s usually messages + book updates + reconciliation.

Bank A ledger
Internal “source of truth” for Bank A customers and positions.
Bank B ledger
Also keeps its own authoritative records.
Reconciliation + investigations + exceptions
Because there are multiple ledgers, banks must ensure they match (eventually).
Trust drivers
Controls + regulation + audits are how society trusts the bank’s records.

2) Crypto currencies: the “shared ledger” idea (before blockchain details)

Space reveal points
From “many ledgers” → to “one shared record”

Traditional finance: each party keeps its own ledger. A shared ledger aims to reduce “multiple versions of truth.”

Traditional many books
Bank A, Bank B, and others maintain separate records, then reconcile.
Shared ledger one view
Participants see the same transaction state (pending/confirmed) without constant reconciliation.
  • Ledger = record of “who owns what” Crypto is a way to maintain that record across many parties.
  • Trust shifts to “rules + validation” Instead of trusting one institution’s ledger, participants trust how updates are validated.
  • “How validation works” comes next We’ll cover how networks synchronize and confirm later — keep it simple for now.
Shared ledger, visualized

Think of a shared “transaction board” everyone reads from.

Participant A
Submits a transaction request.
Shared ledger
Stores the agreed state: pending → confirmed (shared visibility).
Participant B
Reads the same state and acts on it.
Why it matters
Less “who’s right?” and more “we all see the same record.”
Terminology (light)
Ledger • shared state • validation • finality (we’ll unpack later)

3) What does crypto replace in banking… and what does it NOT?

Goal: reduce confusion
Crypto changes how trust and record-keeping happen

It can replace specific system roles (shared ledger + rule-based validation), but banks still provide regulation-facing responsibilities and customer services.

Can replace / reshape
Single “source of truth” ledger role, parts of clearing/settlement, and some reconciliation-heavy steps.
Does NOT replace
Compliance, risk management, customer onboarding, product design, legal accountability.
  • Replaces: “who keeps the record” Shared ledger can become the common record that parties reference.
  • Replaces: parts of clearing & settlement Some flows can settle faster (sometimes “near-instant”) depending on the network model.
  • Banks still own the “trust wrapper” Controls, compliance, risk, and customer protections remain essential.
One sentence to remember

Crypto is not “a bank.”
It’s a different way to build a trusted record and move value — banks still deliver products, compliance, and protection.

Traditional
Trust is institutional + reconciled across separate ledgers.
Crypto-style
Trust is rules + validation on a shared record (plus bank controls around it).
